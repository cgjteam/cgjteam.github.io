# Log 047 --- Classical Proofs and Formal Reconstructions

## The same proposition can have a different proof architecture

The reconstruction of the first propositions of Euclid's Book I has
exposed a distinction that was easy to overlook while the main objective
was simply to obtain compiling Lean proofs.

For a proposition such as I.1, I.2, or I.7 there are at least two
different questions:

1.  Can the proposition be proved from the Hilbert development?
2.  Has Euclid's own proof been reconstructed?

These questions are not equivalent.

The current Book I development primarily answers the first. It
reconstructs Euclidean propositions over the Hilbert foundation and the
Book Zero working layer. In many cases the proof naturally available in
that environment is not Euclid's historical proof.

The difference is not cosmetic. It can change the constructions, the
intermediate objects, and even the logical form of the argument.

## 1. Proposition I.1: intersection versus noncollinearity

Euclid constructs an equilateral triangle on (AB) by drawing two
circles:

``` text
circle centered at A through B
             +
circle centered at B through A
             |
             v
       intersection C
```

The familiar diagram makes the construction look immediate, but the
existence of the intersection point is a genuine foundational issue.

The formal reconstruction takes a different route.

``` text
hilbert_equidistant_point_exists
             |
             v
C with AC ~= AB and BC ~= AB
             |
             v
prove C is not collinear with A,B
```

The metric part is supplied first. The proof must then show that the
witness is a genuine third vertex.

Assuming collinearity, betweenness trichotomy gives the three possible
orders

``` text
A-B-C
B-A-C
A-C-B
```

and each is excluded using

``` text
bookZero_45_partNotEqualWhole
```

because one of the equal segments would have to be a proper part of
another.

Thus the classical and formal proofs solve different intermediate
problems:

``` text
Euclid:          prove the circles have a common point
Formal proof:    prove an equidistant witness is not on the base line
```

The final proposition is the same. The proof architecture is not.

## 2. Proposition I.2: a construction becomes an operation

Euclid's Proposition I.2 is a substantial construction.

Starting from a point (A) and a segment (BC), Euclid uses:

``` text
I.1
 |
equilateral triangle
 |
first circle
 |
produced side
 |
second circle
 |
another produced side
 |
subtraction of equal segments
 |
AL = BC
```

The formal theorem instead uses

``` text
bookZero_49_layoff
```

on a prescribed ray.

At the Book I level the entire classical construction has therefore
become a single reusable synthetic operation.

This is not merely a shorter proof of I.2. It is a change in abstraction
level.

Euclid constructs the mechanism by which a segment can be transported.

Book Zero provides the already established operation:

> lay off a segment congruent to a given segment on a chosen ray.

Once that operation exists, reproducing the original construction inside
every later use would be the wrong proof granularity.

## 3. Proposition I.3: the conclusion is already inside the hypothesis

Proposition I.3 gives an even stronger example.

Classically, given a greater segment (AB) and a smaller segment (CD),
Euclid transports the shorter length and uses a circle to cut the
required part from (AB).

In the formal statement, however, strict segment comparison is
represented by

``` text
HilbertSegmentLess Geo C D A B
```

and this relation already contains a witness (E) such that

``` text
A-E-B
CD ~= AE
```

The Lean proof therefore has the form

``` text
unpack hCDAB
      |
      v
E, A-E-B, CD ~= AE
      |
congruence symmetry
      |
AE ~= CD
```

The classical construction has not merely been replaced by a stronger
theorem. Its existential result has been built into the representation
of the hypothesis.

This illustrates an important principle of formalization:

> The choice of definitions can move mathematical work across theorem
> boundaries.

A theorem may become trivial not because the mathematics disappeared,
but because it was already performed when the language of the theory was
designed.

## 4. Proposition I.4: superposition versus SAS

Euclid's Proposition I.4 proves SAS by superposition.

The argument requires the imagined application of one triangle to
another, including a positional choice for the third vertex.

The Book I Lean theorem instead calls

``` text
TriangleCongruentFromSAS
```

and returns the packaged triangle-congruence result.

Thus:

``` text
Euclid:
SAS hypotheses
   |
superposition
   |
coincidence
   |
remaining side and angles


Lean:
SAS hypotheses
   |
TriangleCongruentFromSAS
   |
remaining side and angles
```

The formal proposition is a wrapper over an already established theorem.

If the research question were specifically "formalize Euclid's proof of
I.4", this would not be sufficient. For the present reconstruction of
Book I over the Hilbert layer, however, it is exactly the appropriate
use of the available theory.

## 5. Proposition I.5: a long construction disappears behind two lemmas

Euclid's proof of the isosceles triangle theorem extends both equal
sides, constructs auxiliary equal segments, joins additional points,
applies SAS twice, and subtracts equal angles.

The formal proof uses two previously established results:

``` text
hilbert_isosceles_base_angles
              |
              v
base angles congruent
              |
hilbert_adjacent_angles_congruent
              |
              v
angles below the base congruent
```

The proposition therefore becomes a composition of two reusable facts.

Again, the classical proof has not been encoded step by step. Its useful
mathematical consequences have already become part of the Hilbert
interface.

## 6. Proposition I.6: contradiction versus ASA

Euclid proves the converse of I.5 by contradiction.

Assume the sides opposite the equal angles are unequal. Cut off a
suitable part from the greater side and use SAS to derive the
contradiction.

The Lean proof instead compares the triangles

``` text
BCA
CBA
```

and applies

``` text
hilbert_asa_sides
```

using the common side (BC) and the two corresponding angle congruences.

The result is direct:

``` text
equal base angles
      +
common side
      |
      v
     ASA
      |
      v
AB ~= AC
```

The classical reductio and its auxiliary construction vanish completely.

## 7. Proposition I.7: positional cases versus uniqueness

Proposition I.7 is particularly revealing because the classical proof is
sensitive to the geometry of the diagram.

The documentation audit showed that the familiar Euclidean argument
treats only one of several possible same-side configurations. A complete
classical analysis requires additional positional cases.

The formal proof takes another route.

From the assumed equal distances it applies SSS to the two triangles
sharing the base. Equality of the relevant angles, together with the
same-side condition, yields a common ray. Uniqueness of segment
construction on that ray then identifies the two candidate vertices.

Schematically:

``` text
AC ~= AD
BC ~= BD
same side of AB
      |
      v
     SSS
      |
equal angles at B
      |
angle uniqueness
      |
C,D on the same ray from B
      |
segment-construction uniqueness
      |
      C = D
```

The formal proof does not solve the classical case analysis more
efficiently. It avoids that proof architecture altogether.

## 8. Proposition I.8: superposition becomes SSS

Euclid proves I.8 by superposing one triangle on the other and invokes
I.7 to exclude a distinct position for the remaining vertex.

The formal theorem applies

``` text
HilbertSSS
```

and extracts the three angle congruences from the resulting triangle
congruence data.

Thus I.8 provides a particularly compact example of proof compression:

``` text
Euclid:
superposition + I.7 -> triangle coincidence -> angles

Lean:
HilbertSSS -> angles
```

The historical dependency on I.7 is no longer a dependency of the formal
Book I theorem.

## 9. Two dependency graphs

These examples suggest that every reconstructed proposition can have two
legitimate dependency graphs.

The first is historical:

``` text
Euclid I.1
   |
Euclid I.2
   |
Euclid I.3
   |
...
```

The second is formal:

``` text
Hilbert axioms
      |
Hilbert interface
      |
Book Zero
      |
selected stronger lemmas
      |
Book I proposition
```

The two graphs need not have the same edges.

This matters when interpreting statements such as

> Proposition I.8 has been formalized.

That statement can mean at least:

``` text
A. The theorem corresponding to I.8 has been proved.

B. Euclid's proof of I.8 has been encoded.

C. A proof with the same historical dependencies has been encoded.
```

The current Book I reconstruction guarantees A.

It often does not claim B or C.

Making that distinction explicit prevents the documentation from
attributing to the Lean source a proof that it does not actually
contain.

## 10. What should the documentation preserve?

The proposition documents now preserve both views rather than forcing
them into a false correspondence.

A useful structure is:

``` text
Euclidean statement
        |
classical proof
        |
classical diagram
        |
Hilbert reconstruction
        |
actual Lean architecture
```

The classical section explains Euclid.

The reconstruction section explains the mathematics used by the formal
proof.

The Lean section shows only those code fragments that reveal an
important interface or proof transition.

This organization allows the difference itself to become visible.

That difference is often more informative than a line-by-line
correspondence would have been.

## 11. Reconstruction as comparison of proof languages

The emerging project can therefore be described more precisely.

It is not only a collection of formalized Euclidean theorems.

It is also a comparison between two languages for organizing synthetic
geometry.

Euclid's language is strongly constructional and sequential:

``` text
construct
join
produce
draw circle
apply previous proposition
derive equality
```

The Hilbert/Book Zero language increasingly uses reusable relational and
uniqueness principles:

``` text
layoff
same ray
segment order
triangle congruence
angle uniqueness
segment uniqueness
```

Neither description alone captures the whole mathematical story.

The comparison shows which pieces of a classical construction become
stable operations when the theory is reorganized axiomatically.

## 12. Consequence for the project

This distinction changes how subsequent Book I propositions should be
documented.

For each proposition we should ask separately:

``` text
What does Euclid prove?
How does Euclid prove it?

What does the Lean theorem state?
How does Lean prove it?

Where do the two architectures coincide?
Where do they diverge?
Which earlier formal theorem absorbs the missing classical work?
```

This is more informative than asking only whether the proposition
compiles.

It also gives the Book Zero experiment a sharper criterion of success.

A good working layer is one that permits classical proof steps to appear
as stable operations without obscuring where their mathematical content
was proved.

## Conclusion

The first propositions of Book I establish a recurring phenomenon:

> Reconstruction of a Euclidean proposition and reconstruction of
> Euclid's proof are different formalization tasks.

The present development reconstructs Book I over a Hilbert foundation
and a Book Zero working language. This often produces proofs that are
shorter, more modular, and structurally different from the classical
arguments.

Those differences are not defects to be hidden.

They are evidence about the organization of synthetic geometry itself.

The next stage of the documentation analysis concerns another source of
such evidence: diagrams. The graphical audit of Book I showed that
diagrams do more than illustrate proofs. They can expose assumptions,
missing cases, and differences between classical and formal proof
architecture.
