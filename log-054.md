# Log 054 --- Euclid Book I as a Test of the Synthetic Geometry Library

## 1. A completed book is more than forty-eight completed propositions

The formal reconstruction of Euclid's Book I has now reached Proposition I.48.

At first sight, the most obvious description of that result is numerical:

```text
48 propositions formalized
```

But this is not the most important outcome.

The more significant result is architectural.

Book I has acted as a sustained test of the synthetic geometry library. It has forced the project to distinguish between several mathematical layers that are easy to blur together in an informal development:

```text
source axioms
derived plane theory
working synthetic interface
classical Euclidean constructions
proposition-local arguments
```

The resulting implementation is therefore not merely a collection of forty-eight theorem statements. It is a record of how classical synthetic geometry becomes organized when one tries to formalize a complete classical corpus rather than a single isolated proof.

The central lesson of Book I is that the useful working language of geometry does not coincide with the foundational axiom layer.

Axioms provide the logical ground.

Proofs require a developed mathematical language above them.

Book I is the first sufficiently large corpus in the project to make that distinction visible at scale.

## 2. The current Hilbert route

The recent refactor of the Hilbert foundation makes this structure explicit.

The active planar route is now organized as

```text
HilbertCore
    |
HilbertAxioms
    |
HilbertPlaneAPI
    |
HilbertPlaneTheory
    |
HilbertGrundlagen
    |
HilbertInterface
    |
HilbertBookZero
    |
Euclid Book I
```

The layers serve different purposes.

`HilbertCore.lean` supplies the primitive representation of points, lines, incidence, betweenness, segment congruence, and primitive angle congruence.

`HilbertAxioms.lean` now records Hilbert's five axiom groups in a source-faithful form.

`HilbertPlaneAPI.lean` transports the new source-oriented foundation into the working planar vocabulary required by the established theorem corpus.

`HilbertPlaneTheory.lean` contains the derived planar mathematics.

`HilbertGrundlagen.lean`, `HilbertInterface.lean`, and `HilbertBookZero.lean` provide increasingly convenient synthetic language for use by Euclidean propositions.

The frozen declarations retained in `HilbertAxiomsWork.lean` are compatibility support. They are not a second foundational theory.

This separation is important because Book I depends mostly on derived mathematics, not directly on the source axioms.

The proposition files therefore do not need to repeat the complete module graph. Their dependency summaries can begin at the mathematically relevant working layer.

This is not merely a documentation convention. It reflects the real mathematical structure of the implementation.

## 3. Euclid's proof, Euclid's theorem, and the Lean proof

The reconstruction repeatedly showed that three objects must be distinguished:

```text
Euclid's historical proof
Euclid's mathematical proposition
the Lean proof used in the library
```

These are related, but they are not identical.

A formal proof can establish the same proposition without reproducing Euclid's historical route line by line.

Sometimes the formalization follows the classical argument closely.

Sometimes it uses a stronger theorem already available in the library.

Sometimes a local construction in Euclid has become a reusable theorem in the developed Hilbert layer.

For example, a proof that Euclid organizes as a local SAS argument may become shorter in Lean because a derived SSS theorem is already available.

A local appeal to a diagram may become an explicit theorem about betweenness, line separation, or intersection.

A repeated parallelogram argument may become a single reusable theorem in the interface.

This difference should not be treated as a defect.

It is one of the main phenomena exposed by formalization.

A classical text presents propositions sequentially.

A formal library reorganizes reusable mathematics into a dependency graph.

The formal proof therefore answers a different architectural question:

> Which previously established results are the right reusable components for proving this proposition now?

That question is not identical to:

> Which earlier propositions did Euclid cite in the original text?

Book I demonstrates this distinction repeatedly.

## 4. From sequence to dependency graph

Euclid's Book I is presented as a sequence:

```text
I.1
I.2
I.3
...
I.48
```

The Lean implementation reveals a richer structure.

Some propositions are construction theorems.

Some establish reusable congruence tools.

Some create order-theoretic infrastructure.

Some develop parallel theory.

Some introduce parallelogram reasoning.

Some require a new language for equality of figures.

The result is better represented as a directed dependency graph than as a simple linear sequence.

This is one of the strongest methodological conclusions of the project.

Formalization does not merely verify that each proposition follows from earlier assumptions.

It reveals which mathematical ideas are genuinely reusable and which arguments remain proposition-specific.

The distinction is visible in the file architecture.

Results that recur across many propositions migrate into common modules.

Arguments that are specific to one Euclidean configuration remain in the corresponding proposition file.

Over time, the library therefore becomes an atlas of reusable synthetic structures rather than a transcription of a textbook.

## 5. The first major phase: neutral geometry

A large part of early Book I belongs to neutral geometry.

The central ingredients are incidence, order, congruence, triangle constructions, and elementary comparison of segments and angles.

The early propositions test whether the formal system can support ordinary synthetic reasoning without prematurely invoking Euclid's parallel axiom.

This is a significant test.

A formal library can easily become stronger than intended if powerful results are introduced too early or if hidden Euclidean assumptions enter through convenience lemmas.

The reconstruction of the early propositions therefore serves as an audit of the neutral layer.

The project has repeatedly examined the axiomatic status of derived results.

This distinction becomes especially important around Proposition I.29.

## 6. Proposition I.29 as a structural boundary

Proposition I.29 marks a genuine transition.

Before I.29, much of the development belongs to neutral geometry.

At I.29, the Euclidean theory of parallels becomes active.

The importance of this proposition is therefore not only local.

It changes the logical strength of the working environment.

After I.29, the library can use a developed Euclidean parallel theory to prove statements about alternate angles, corresponding angles, parallelograms, and related constructions.

This makes later proofs shorter and more modular.

The formal development therefore confirms a classical structural fact:

> The parallel theory is not merely another topic in Book I. It changes the logical regime in which the later propositions operate.

This boundary is clearly visible in the Lean architecture.

## 7. Parallelograms as a reusable theory

One of the strongest examples of architectural emergence in Book I is the theory of parallelograms.

What begins in Euclid as a sequence of proposition-specific observations develops into a reusable formal module.

The library now contains results concerning, among other things:

```text
opposite-side parallelism
opposite-side congruence
diagonal intersection
midpoint structure
angle transfer
parallelogram recognition
```

These theorems are then reused by later propositions instead of being re-proved locally.

This is exactly the sort of transformation that formalization makes visible.

A classical proof text can afford to repeat a familiar argument.

A formal library benefits from identifying its reusable mathematical core.

The theory of parallelograms therefore became one of the first substantial examples of a local Euclidean proof pattern turning into an independent component of the library.

## 8. Midpoints, medians, and the value of derived constructions

A similar phenomenon occurs with midpoint constructions.

The midpoint needed in Euclid I.10 is not represented in the source axiom layer as a primitive axiom.

It is a derived neutral theorem in the developed plane theory.

That distinction matters.

The current architecture places the source axioms in `HilbertAxioms.lean`, while the substantial synthetic midpoint construction belongs to the derived planar layer.

The proposition-level theorem can therefore be very short.

This is an important example of how the library should work.

A classical construction may require a long foundational proof once.

After that proof is extracted into the reusable theory, later propositions should use the result as a mathematical operation.

Book I repeatedly confirms this pattern.

The formalization becomes clearer when difficult constructions are proved once at the appropriate layer and then treated as stable tools.

## 9. Book Zero as a working synthetic layer

The reconstruction of Book I also tested the role of Book Zero.

The Beeson--Narboux--Wiedijk material provides a large collection of elementary synthetic results that are logically above the Hilbert foundation but below the classical Euclidean propositions.

This layer proved useful because many proposition proofs require more than raw axioms but less than a large specialized theory.

Typical working tools include:

```text
segment constructions
angle constructions
elementary betweenness lemmas
ray and line manipulations
triangle congruence consequences
basic intersection results
```

Book Zero therefore acts as a bridge between foundational geometry and proposition-level reconstruction.

Book I is the first large corpus in the project that tests whether this bridge is practical.

The answer is broadly positive.

The layer is not complete in any final sense, but it is useful enough to support a substantial classical development.

## 10. Proposition I.35 and the second major boundary

Proposition I.35 introduces a different kind of difficulty.

The earlier development is dominated by incidence, order, congruence, and parallelism.

At I.35, the subject changes to equality of rectilinear figures.

This cannot be represented faithfully by ordinary segment or angle congruence.

The formal reconstruction therefore required a new language.

The project introduced a synthetic scissors and equicomplementability framework rather than immediately assigning numerical areas to figures.

This is an important architectural decision.

The transition can be summarized as

```text
segment and angle congruence
        |
triangle and parallelogram geometry
        |
equality of rectilinear figures
        |
scissors / equicomplementability
```

Proposition I.35 therefore marks a second structural boundary in Book I.

It shows that a geometry library eventually needs notions that describe transformations of whole figures, not only relations between individual segments and angles.

The subsequent area propositions can then reuse that framework.

## 11. Why a scissors layer was useful

The scissors formalization is deliberately modest.

It does not attempt to build a complete measure theory for polygons.

Instead, it records the combinatorial and synthetic operations actually needed by the Euclidean proofs.

This is consistent with the general philosophy of the project:

> Introduce the weakest reusable structure that the proof corpus actually demands.

The benefit is twofold.

First, the formalization remains close to the synthetic character of Euclid's argument.

Second, it avoids introducing numerical area machinery before the corpus requires it.

Book I therefore provides another example of architecture emerging from proof analysis rather than being imposed in advance.

## 12. Diagrams as proof analysis

The graphical audit of Book I produced a further methodological result.

Diagrams are not merely illustrations.

In difficult propositions they expose logical information that is easy to miss in prose.

A diagram can reveal:

```text
which points are assumed distinct
which point lies between which others
which lines are required to intersect
which cases depend on different positional configurations
which construction belongs only to one case
```

This became especially important in propositions involving complicated intersections, parallelograms, or equality of figures.

The formal development therefore uses diagrams as a tool for proof analysis.

This changes their role.

The question is no longer only:

> Does this picture illustrate the theorem?

The stronger question is:

> Does this picture accurately represent the configuration required by the formal proof?

That distinction is important in Hilbert-style geometry, where positional conditions must be stated explicitly.

The Book I graphical audit showed that apparently harmless classical diagrams can conceal substantial assumptions.

## 13. Hidden case distinctions

One recurring difference between classical and formal arguments is the treatment of cases.

A textbook diagram often shows one generic configuration.

A formal proof must account for every configuration permitted by the hypotheses.

This exposes hidden branches.

Points may occur in different orders.

Segments may overlap, meet at an endpoint, or be disjoint.

An auxiliary intersection may exist only in one branch.

A construction that appears universal in the classical diagram may in fact depend on a positional assumption.

The formalization of Book I repeatedly turned such implicit distinctions into explicit case analyses.

This is not bureaucratic overhead.

It is mathematical information.

The cases describe the real logical domain of the theorem.

## 14. Representation choices matter

The implementation also shows that formal representation is mathematically consequential.

Segments are not merely pairs of points in an ordered tuple.

Angles are not merely three-point expressions.

Lines have both primitive and extensional roles.

Collinearity can be represented through a line witness.

Parallelism must distinguish actual coplanar parallel lines from unrelated disjoint lines in higher-dimensional settings.

Such choices affect which facts are definitional, which require axioms, and which require derived theorems.

The recent Hilbert refactor made this especially visible.

The source-faithful primitive layer and the working planar API do not have identical shapes.

A compatibility layer is therefore necessary.

Book I sits above that boundary and tests whether the chosen representation supports practical proof development.

## 15. Source fidelity and production usability

The project now explicitly separates two legitimate goals.

The first is source fidelity.

For Hilbert's axioms, this means representing the original axiom groups as accurately as possible.

The second is production usability.

For Book I, this means having a convenient synthetic language in which ordinary Euclidean arguments can be expressed without constantly unfolding the foundational layer.

These goals should not be conflated.

A source-faithful axiom file should not be forced to contain the entire developed theory.

A convenient interface should not be mistaken for the foundational system itself.

The current architecture reflects that distinction.

Book I provides strong evidence that both layers are necessary.

## 16. The importance of compatibility during refactoring

The recent refactor of the Hilbert axiom layer also produced a practical lesson in formal library engineering.

A mature theorem corpus should not be rewritten merely because the foundation has been reorganized.

Instead, compatibility layers can preserve the established working API while a more faithful foundation is introduced underneath.

This approach allowed the project to keep the existing planar theory while replacing the older mixed foundational organization.

The principle is general:

> A formal library should distinguish mathematical refactoring from unnecessary downstream churn.

Book I benefited from this separation because proposition proofs could remain focused on geometry rather than on representational migration.

## 17. The complete Book I as an integration test

A single theorem can compile even when the surrounding architecture is poorly organized.

A complete classical book is a much stronger test.

Book I exercises many different parts of the library:

```text
incidence
order
segment congruence
angle congruence
triangle congruence
construction
parallel theory
perpendicularity
midpoints
parallelograms
figure equality
scissors reasoning
```

The fact that these components must coexist exposes interface problems that isolated examples do not reveal.

For this reason, the completed Book I can be viewed as an integration test for the geometry library.

It does not prove that the architecture is final.

It does show that the current layers are capable of supporting a large and varied synthetic corpus.

## 18. The later propositions as stress tests

The final part of Book I is especially useful because it combines structures developed earlier.

By the time the reconstruction reaches the closing propositions, the proofs no longer test only one local theorem.

They test whether several previously developed theories interact coherently.

The Pythagorean proposition and its converse are natural examples.

They require the library to combine substantial Euclidean geometry with the content machinery developed earlier.

The fact that the final propositions can be expressed within the accumulated interface is evidence that the earlier architectural extractions were not merely local conveniences.

They support later mathematics.

## 19. What Book I does not prove

It is important not to overstate the conclusion.

Book I does not determine the final architecture of synthetic geometry.

Forty-eight propositions are substantial, but they represent only one classical corpus.

Other parts of Euclid, Hilbert, Coxeter, Wyler, Smith, and higher-dimensional geometry impose different structural demands.

The project already shows this in the spatial branch.

The three-dimensional and higher-dimensional developments require explicit planes, spatial incidence, coplanarity, and distinctions that do not appear in the same way in Book I.

Likewise, continuity and deeper metric constructions are not exhausted by the first book.

The correct conclusion is therefore limited:

> Book I is a large and meaningful architectural test, not a final specification of geometry.

This distinction should remain explicit.

## 20. A methodological conclusion

The formal reconstruction changes the way one can read Euclid.

The propositions are no longer only a sequence of classical results.

They become probes into the structure of geometric reasoning.

Each proposition can ask:

```text
What is primitive?
What is derived?
What is reusable?
What is configuration-specific?
What depends on order?
What depends on congruence?
Where does Euclidean parallelism first enter?
When does figure equality require a new formal language?
Which assumptions are hidden by the diagram?
```

These questions are difficult to answer systematically in an informal proof corpus.

Lean forces them into the open.

This is one of the principal intellectual gains of the project.

## 21. From proof verification to geometry library design

The project began with individual synthetic proofs.

Book I shows the next stage.

Formalization becomes a method of library design.

Repeated proof patterns become definitions.

Repeated lemmas become theorem families.

Logical boundaries become module boundaries.

Classical constructions become reusable operations.

Hidden diagram assumptions become explicit hypotheses.

The result is not simply a verified transcription of Euclid.

It is a growing synthetic geometry library whose architecture is extracted from the mathematics itself.

That is the broader significance of the Book I reconstruction.

## 22. Current picture

The present state can be summarized schematically as follows:

```text
Hilbert source axioms
        |
derived planar geometry
        |
working synthetic interface
        |
Book Zero constructions
        |
Euclid Book I
        |
reusable theories extracted from the corpus
        |
future Euclid / Wyler / Coxeter / higher-dimensional work
```

The arrow is not one-directional in practice.

Later propositions force improvements in lower layers.

Documentation audits reveal hidden assumptions.

Refactors separate source fidelity from compatibility infrastructure.

New geometric domains expose distinctions that were invisible in the plane.

The library therefore develops iteratively.

Book I is not the end of that process.

It is the first point at which the process can be observed across a complete classical book.

## 23. Conclusion

The most important result of formalizing Euclid's Book I is not the number forty-eight.

It is the emergence of a layered synthetic geometry architecture.

The reconstruction has shown that a practical formal geometry library needs a distinction between:

```text
primitive representation
source axioms
derived theory
working interface
classical constructions
proposition-specific proofs
```

It has also shown that classical proof structure and formal library structure are related but not identical.

Euclid supplies the mathematical corpus.

Hilbert clarifies the logical foundation.

Book Zero supplies a useful intermediate synthetic language.

Lean reveals the dependency graph.

The diagrams reveal hidden configuration data.

The proposition sequence tests whether the resulting architecture actually works.

The completed Book I should therefore be understood as an architectural experiment in formal synthetic geometry.

The forty-eight verified propositions are the visible output.

The deeper result is the mathematical structure that had to emerge underneath them.
