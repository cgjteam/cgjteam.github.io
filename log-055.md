# Log 055 --- Euclid Book II as a Test of Synthetic Content Geometry

## 1. A different kind of Euclidean book

The completion of Book II changes the character of the formalization.

Book I is dominated by constructions, incidence, order, congruence,
parallelism, parallelograms, and the gradual development of a working
synthetic geometry.

Book II uses that geometry in a different way.  Its propositions are often
written as identities involving rectangles and squares.  In a modern
presentation they can look almost algebraic.

The formal reconstruction deliberately avoids replacing those propositions by
ordinary algebra.

There is no numerical area function at the center of the development, and no
segment field is introduced merely to rewrite Euclid's statements as polynomial
identities.

Instead, the formalization remains synthetic.

The basic objects are still geometric configurations, but the new working
language is a language of rectangles, squares, finite dissection, transport,
and equicomplementability.

That makes Book II the first sustained test of whether the geometry library can
support a synthetic theory of content rather than only a synthetic theory of
incidence and congruence.

## 2. The foundation is still geometric

The active planar route remains

```text
HilbertCore
    |
HilbertAxioms
    |
HilbertPlaneAPI
    |
HilbertPlaneTheory
    |
HilbertGrundlagen
    |
HilbertInterface
    |
HilbertBookZero
    |
Euclid Book I
    |
Book II working infrastructure
```

The role of Book II is not to introduce another foundational geometry.

It is a client of the already developed plane theory.

The source-faithful Hilbert axioms remain in `HilbertAxioms.lean`.
Derived planar mathematics remains in `HilbertPlaneTheory.lean`.
The compatibility layer remains in `HilbertPlaneAPI.lean`.
The working synthetic language is supplied by the higher Hilbert interfaces,
Book Zero, and the completed Book I development.

Book II then adds specialized content machinery on top of this structure.

## 3. The new working language

Several modules become central in Book II:

```text
HilbertRectangle
HilbertScissors
HilbertScissorsEquicomplementable
HilbertSquareTransport
HilbertAngleDecomposition
HilbertRightAngle
```

Together with the already established Book I theory, they provide a language
in which Euclid's geometric identities can be stated without introducing
numerical area.

A typical Book II argument has the form

```text
geometric construction
        |
        v
rectangle or square configuration
        |
        v
finite decomposition
        |
        v
transport of congruent pieces
        |
        v
scissors equality or equicomplementability
```

This is the main structural difference from Book I.

## 4. Exact scissors equality

The first content relation is

```text
HilbertScissorsEq
```

It expresses exact finite cut-and-paste equality.

This is already visible in Proposition II.1.

The production theorem

```text
rectangle_split_scissorsEq
```

states that a rectangle with an interior cut is scissors-equal to the sum of
the two pieces determined by that cut.

Its proof reuses the dissection theorem developed earlier in Proposition I.47.

This is a characteristic Book II phenomenon: a theorem that historically
belongs to the Pythagorean part of Book I becomes reusable infrastructure for
rectangle identities in Book II.

The historical name of the reused helper still refers to a square split, but
the formal theorem actually needs only parallelogram structure.  This is a good
example of formalization revealing a more general reusable statement beneath a
source-specific proof step.

## 5. From II.1 to a rectangle calculus

Propositions II.1--II.8 develop a substantial exact scissors calculus.

The public conclusions are formulated at the level of exact finite
cut-and-paste equality.

The propositions reuse each other increasingly often.

For example:

```text
II.1
  |
  +--> II.2
  |
  +--> II.3
        |
        v
       II.4
        |
        +--> II.5
        +--> II.7
              |
              v
             II.8
```

The actual dependency graph is richer than this schematic picture, but the
important fact is that Book II quickly stops being a sequence of isolated
propositions.

It becomes a small theory of synthetic rectangle identities.

## 6. The role of Book I

Book II depends heavily on the completed Book I library.

The dependencies are not merely historical references.

Later propositions directly reuse results such as:

```text
I.6
I.10
I.12
I.16
I.19
I.20
I.32
I.34
I.45
I.46
I.47
```

Different propositions use different subsets.

This means that Book II tests whether the results of Book I have really become
reusable library theorems.

A proposition such as II.9 is therefore not built from a new Book II axiom
system.  Its proof is assembled from existing angle theory, Book I geometry,
square transport, and content bookkeeping.

## 7. Proposition II.9 and orientation

II.9 exposed an important formal issue that is easy to hide in an informal
diagram.

The production development contains two theorems:

```text
euclid_proposition_2_9_oriented
euclid_proposition_2_9
```

The first theorem handles one oriented order configuration.

The second is the public two-orientation wrapper.

The wrapper accepts either orientation of the relevant point configuration,
reduces the second case to the oriented theorem by swapping endpoints, and then
transports the square representatives back to the public orientation.

This is a useful example of the difference between

```text
the geometric core of a proof
```

and

```text
the complete public theorem interface
```

The oriented theorem captures the main geometry.

The wrapper closes the symmetry that Euclid leaves largely implicit in the
diagram.

## 8. A second content relation

The later part of Book II uses a broader relation:

```text
HilbertScissorsEquicomplementable
```

This is the correct level when the argument allows common additions or
removals rather than only a direct dissection into the same finite collection
of pieces.

The distinction between

```text
exact scissors equality
```

and

```text
equicomplementability
```

is essential.

It prevents the formalization from silently treating every classical
"equality of figures" as one undifferentiated primitive relation.

In the current reconstruction the public boundary changes after II.8:
II.1--II.8 are stated using exact scissors equality, while II.9--II.14 use the
equicomplementability layer.

That boundary is one of the main architectural results of Book II.

## 9. II.9 and II.10 return to source-faithful geometry

Propositions II.9 and II.10 are especially revealing because they do not simply
form a chain from the preceding Book II identities.

Their production files return directly to a substantial collection of Book I
results and specialized geometric infrastructure.

The proof architecture includes:

```text
construction
angle decomposition
right-angle reasoning
Book I parallel and triangle results
Pythagorean decomposition
square transport
scissors bookkeeping
```

This shows that the dependency graph of Book II is not simply

```text
II.1 -> II.2 -> ... -> II.14
```

Euclid's textual order remains important, but the formal library exposes a much
richer graph.

## 10. II.11--II.14 combine both routes

The final propositions combine the two kinds of dependency.

They reuse earlier Book II identities and at the same time call directly into
Book I and the reusable Hilbert content infrastructure.

For example, II.11 imports material from II.2, II.3, and II.6 together with
midpoint, order, construction, Pythagorean, square-transport, and rectangle
machinery.

II.12 combines the Book II decomposition from II.4 with right-angle geometry
and Book I.

II.13 builds on II.12 and II.7.

II.14 uses II.13 and II.5 together with the late Book I construction and
Pythagorean results.

The closing part of Book II is therefore a genuine integration test.

## 11. Rectangle transport and square transport

A major source of proof compression is transport.

In informal geometry one often says that two rectangles have the same sides,
or that two squares are on congruent segments, and then proceeds as if their
content equality were immediate.

The formal library makes those steps explicit.

`HilbertRectangle` supplies rectangle-level infrastructure.

`HilbertSquareTransport` supplies reusable transport of square representatives.

This has two advantages.

First, proposition proofs no longer need to rebuild the same congruence and
content arguments locally.

Second, the formalization separates geometric congruence from equality of
content.

Those are related notions, but they are not definitionally identical.

## 12. Why no numerical area was needed

A modern algebraic proof of Book II can be very short.

For example, one may identify a rectangle with a product of lengths and then
use ordinary algebra.

That route is mathematically legitimate, but it answers a different question.

The present project asks whether Euclid's Book II can be reconstructed inside
a synthetic geometry library while preserving the geometric meaning of the
figures and decompositions.

The completed Book II shows that a large part of the classical algebraic
appearance can be recovered through:

```text
finite decomposition
congruence
transport
addition of figure terms
equicomplementability
```

without first replacing figures by numbers.

## 13. Representation choices become visible

Book II also makes representation choices more visible than Book I.

A square or rectangle is not merely a predicate on four points.

It also appears as a term in a content expression.

The order of vertices, the chosen representative, and the transport between
congruent representatives can therefore matter at the formal level even when
the classical diagram treats them as interchangeable.

This is one reason why square-transport lemmas are not cosmetic helpers.

They are part of the representation boundary between geometry and the content
calculus.

## 14. Diagrams remain proof analysis tools

The Book II diagrams are not only illustrations.

They help identify:

```text
betweenness relations
which side is extended
which rectangle is being cut
which square representative is used
which orientation a theorem expects
which pieces are common to two content expressions
```

The II.9 orientation issue is a particularly clear example.

A diagram can make one orientation look canonical even though the public
theorem must support both.

The graphical audit therefore remains part of proof analysis, just as it was in
Book I.

## 15. Source fidelity and library reuse

As in Book I, three objects must be distinguished:

```text
Euclid's historical proof
Euclid's mathematical proposition
the Lean proof used in the library
```

Book II makes this distinction even sharper.

The formal proof may reuse a general rectangle split, a square transport lemma,
or a content normalization theorem that has no literal counterpart as a named
step in Euclid.

This does not necessarily make the proof less synthetic.

It means that the library has extracted reusable mathematical operations from
earlier arguments.

A source-faithful reconstruction therefore does not require a mechanical
line-by-line transcription.

It requires control over which mathematical transformations are being used and
how they relate to the classical proof.

## 16. The Hilbert refactor survived Book II

The recent Hilbert refactor changed the foundational architecture substantially.

The source-faithful axiom layer is now separated from the legacy plane API and
from the derived plane theorem corpus.

Book II did not require its proposition proofs to be rewritten around those
foundational changes.

That is significant.

It shows that the higher interfaces and the Book I/content infrastructure act
as a real abstraction boundary.

The proposition files remain clients of a stable working theory rather than
clients of the exact current representation of Hilbert's axioms.

## 17. Documentation as part of the audit

The Book II documentation has now been synchronized with the current
architecture.

The complete volume covers II.1--II.14.

Its introduction records the current Hilbert dependency route and distinguishes
the foundational module graph from the local mathematical dependency summaries
inside proposition chapters.

The proposition documentation was also cross-checked against the production
Lean files.

In particular, the II.9 documentation was corrected to reflect the current
two-layer theorem architecture:

```text
euclid_proposition_2_9_oriented
euclid_proposition_2_9
```

rather than describing only the oriented theorem.

## 18. Compilation as a documentation test

The documentation is treated as an executable technical artifact.

Each proposition has an individual wrapper

```text
Proposition2_1.tex
...
Proposition2_14.tex
```

and the whole volume is assembled by

```text
Book2R.tex
```

The compilation script now checks every proposition document and then compiles
`Book2R.tex` twice.

This serves a similar purpose to `lake build` on the Lean side.

A documentation change is not complete merely because one edited file looks
plausible.

The complete document graph must still compile.

## 19. What Book II has tested

The completion of Book II tests several parts of the library simultaneously.

It tests whether:

```text
Book I results are reusable,
rectangle geometry is stable,
right-angle infrastructure composes,
square representatives can be transported,
finite-scissors equality scales beyond one proposition,
equicomplementability is usable as a public relation,
orientation cases can be isolated cleanly,
and the documentation can track the real Lean dependency graph.
```

This is much more than the formalization of fourteen additional propositions.

## 20. What Book II does not yet provide

The completed reconstruction does not claim to be a complete theory of area.

It does not introduce a general numerical measure of polygons.

It does not identify content with a real-valued invariant.

It does not yet prove that every classical area argument should be represented
by the current scissors/equicomplementability language.

Those are separate questions.

The present result is more specific:

> Euclid's Book II can be reconstructed as a coherent synthetic theory of
> rectangle and square content using the existing Hilbert/Book I geometry and
> a finite-scissors/equicomplementability layer.

## 21. From Book I to Book II

The two completed books test different aspects of the project.

Book I tests whether a synthetic geometry library can support the classical
construction and theorem sequence of elementary Euclidean geometry.

Book II tests whether that same library can support a sustained theory of
geometric content.

The transition can be summarized as:

```text
Book I
construction
incidence
order
congruence
parallelism
parallelograms
Pythagorean geometry
        |
        v
Book II
rectangles
squares
decomposition
transport
scissors equality
equicomplementability
```

The second book therefore does not merely continue the first.

It changes the type of mathematical structure being stressed.

## 22. Conclusion

With Propositions II.1--II.14 completed, Book II becomes a second large-scale
integration test of the geometry library.

The main architectural result is not an algebraic encoding of Euclid.

It is the emergence of a synthetic content language built from already
formalized geometry.

Book I supplied the geometric machinery.

Book II forced that machinery to interact with rectangles, squares, finite
decomposition, transport, and equicomplementability across a complete
classical book.

That makes the completion of Book II an important boundary in the project:
the library is no longer being tested only on geometric existence and
congruence arguments, but also on a sustained synthetic calculus of content.
