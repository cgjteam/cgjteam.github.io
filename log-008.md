# Log 008 - What the Finlay Proof Does Not Use

## Negative Information from Dependency Analysis

### Introduction

Formalization shows not only which ingredients occur in a proof, but also which familiar mathematical structures do not occur on the dependency path being studied.

This negative information must be interpreted carefully.

If a notion does not appear in the final Finlay argument, this does not automatically mean that it is absent from the underlying geometry or unnecessary for proving the reusable theorems invoked by Finlay.

It means only that the final proof does not call upon that notion directly.

The purpose of this log is to record this narrower and more useful observation.

---

## Proof Dependencies and Background Theory

A classical geometry proof is read inside a rich mathematical environment.

A triangle immediately suggests lengths, angles, similarity, area, coordinates, transformations, and order on lines.

The formal proof allows us to ask a more precise question:

> Which of these notions occur explicitly in the dependency path of the final argument?

This question is different from asking which notions are required by the entire geometric theory.

That distinction is essential for interpreting the Finlay formalization correctly.

---

## No Coordinate Calculation

The Finlay proof does not use a coordinate representation of points.

There are no calculations with pairs such as

```text
(x, y)
```

and no equations of lines or slope computations in the proof.

The argument is expressed synthetically through geometric relations and reusable theorems.

This shows that coordinates are not part of the final proof strategy.

It does not imply that coordinate models are irrelevant to the semantics or metatheory of the geometry.

---

## No Explicit Metric Calculation

The final argument does not calculate distances or lengths numerically.

Midpoint information is consumed through the available geometric interface rather than by evaluating a distance function.

This is a useful distinction.

The proof uses midpoint relations, but the logical origin of those relations belongs to the underlying theory and must be analyzed separately.

Thus the absence of explicit metric calculation in `Finlay` should not be confused with the absence of congruence or metric information from the foundations used to establish earlier results.

---

## Angle Reasoning Is Hidden Below the Final Proof

The final Finlay theorem does not explicitly manipulate angles.

At the level of the final argument, the Midsegment Theorem supplies the required parallel relations directly.

However, this does not show that angle theory is absent from the development.

The proof of the Midsegment Theorem may itself depend on congruence and angle relations.

The correct conclusion is therefore narrower:

> angle reasoning is not visible in the final Finlay proof because it has been encapsulated in reusable results used at a higher level.

This is an important example of how abstraction changes the apparent dependency structure of a formal proof.

---

## Similarity Is Not Used by This Route

Many classical treatments of the centroid use similar triangles and derive the familiar ratio

$$
2:1.
$$

Finlay's argument follows a different route.

Its visible structure is approximately:

```text
midpoint information
        |
        v
midsegment theorem
        |
        v
parallel relations
        |
        v
parallelogram
        |
        v
diagonal bisection
        |
        v
third median
```

No similarity theorem is invoked along this route.

This is a genuine mathematical feature of the chosen proof rather than a statement about the whole geometry library.

---

## Betweenness Requires More Care

The original version of this analysis suggested that Finlay's proof did not require order or betweenness.

That conclusion is too strong.

The final theorem may not expose betweenness prominently, but some of the geometric results used below it can depend on order information.

Moreover, the distinction between collinearity and betweenness belongs to the foundational analysis of the geometry and cannot be settled merely by inspecting the surface form of `Finlay`.

The safe conclusion is therefore:

> the final proof is written largely in terms of higher-level collinearity, midpoint, parallelism, and parallelogram interfaces; the role of betweenness must be determined by tracing those interfaces back to their foundations.

This is precisely the kind of question that motivated the reverse-engineering stage of the project.

---

## Construction and Existence

Finlay's geometric argument introduces auxiliary points.

In a paper proof this may be described informally as a construction.

In Lean, however, it is important to distinguish between:

1. a theorem asserting the existence of a point with specified properties,
2. assumptions supplying such a point,
3. a constructive procedure producing the point.

These are logically different situations.

The formalization therefore forces us to ask not merely whether an auxiliary point appears, but how its existence enters the proof.

This question belongs to dependency analysis rather than to the geometric diagram alone.

---

## What Negative Information Actually Tells Us

The useful lesson is not that Finlay has revealed a complete minimal foundation for geometry.

It has not.

Instead, the formal proof reveals which mathematical structures are visible at a particular level of abstraction.

At the level of the final argument, the main mechanisms are:

- midpoint information,
- collinearity,
- parallel relations,
- parallelogram recognition,
- diagonal bisection,
- median identification.

Lower-level ingredients can disappear from view once they have been encapsulated inside reusable theorems.

Negative information must therefore always be indexed by level:

```text
not used explicitly in Finlay
            !=
not used in its supporting theorems
            !=
not required by the underlying geometry
```

This distinction is one of the main results of the present analysis.

---

## Consequences for Library Development

Dependency analysis can guide library design, but it should not be used to declare concepts unnecessary too early.

A better principle is:

> Introduce and classify geometric structures according to their logical role, and distinguish direct proof dependencies from dependencies hidden behind reusable interfaces.

This allows high-level synthetic proofs to remain concise without losing information about their foundations.

It also prevents the architecture of the entire library from being inferred from a single theorem.

---

## Outlook

The Finlay proof provides a useful test case for studying abstraction boundaries.

The next stage is to continue tracing high-level results back through their interfaces and determine which lower-level assumptions actually support them.

The objective is therefore not merely to list what one proof uses or does not use.

It is to understand how mathematical information disappears and reappears as one moves between different layers of a formal geometry library.
