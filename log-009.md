# Log 009 - The Dependency Graph of a Synthetic Proof

## Reading Finlay Through Its Dependencies

### Introduction

The previous logs began to separate several questions that had initially been mixed together:

- which notions occur in the Finlay development,
- which of them are primitive or defined,
- which dependencies are visible in the final proof,
- which dependencies are hidden behind reusable interfaces.

The next step is to look at the proof as a dependency structure.

A formal proof is not merely a sequence of commands. It is a network in which high-level geometric statements depend on lower-level results, which in turn depend on definitions and axioms.

The Finlay proof provides a small enough example for this structure to be inspected directly.

---

## The Visible Dependency Path

At the level of the final synthetic argument, the main route can be summarized schematically as:

```text
midpoint information
        |
        v
Midsegment Theorem
        |
        v
parallel relations
        |
        v
parallelogram recognition
        |
        v
diagonal bisection
        |
        v
new midpoint
        |
        v
third median
```

This graph describes the mathematical organization visible from the final proof.

It should not be confused with the complete foundational dependency graph of the library. Each node can hide further definitions, lemmas, and axiomatic dependencies.

That distinction is essential.

---

## Step 1 - Midpoint Information Produces Parallel Relations

The first major transition is provided by the Midsegment Theorem.

Midpoint hypotheses are converted into parallel relations.

Schematically:

```text
midpoint information
        |
        v
MidsegmentTheorem
        |
        v
parallelism
```

The proof then transports these parallel relations along appropriate collinearities.

Thus the midpoint data does not lead directly to the desired third median. It first creates the parallel configuration needed for the auxiliary quadrilateral.

---

## Step 2 - Parallel Relations Produce a Parallelogram

The next transition combines two pairs of parallel sides:

```text
parallel relations
        |
        v
parallelogram recognition
        |
        v
parallelogram structure
```

At this point the argument changes geometric setting.

The original problem concerns medians of a triangle, but the proof temporarily moves into the theory of parallelograms.

This is one of the central structural features of Finlay's construction.

---

## Step 3 - The Parallelogram Produces a New Midpoint

The crucial property of the auxiliary parallelogram is diagonal bisection.

Schematically:

```text
parallelogram
        |
        v
diagonal intersection and bisection
        |
        v
midpoint of BC
```

This is the mechanism by which the auxiliary construction returns information to the original triangle.

The parallelogram is therefore not merely a convenient diagrammatic object. Its diagonal structure produces the midpoint required for the third median.

---

## Step 4 - The New Midpoint Identifies the Third Median

Once the relevant point is known to be the midpoint of \(BC\), the line through \(A\) and that point is identified as a median.

The remaining collinearity information places the previously constructed intersection point \(G\) on this median.

Thus the proof closes by returning from parallelogram geometry to the original statement about the triangle.

---

## What the Graph Hides

The visible graph is already useful, but it is not the whole logical story.

For example, a node such as

```lean
MidsegmentTheorem
```

encapsulates its own proof and therefore its own dependency graph.

Likewise, the results used for parallelogram recognition and diagonal bisection may depend on lower-level incidence, order, congruence, or parallelism principles.

Consequently, a short high-level proof can rest on a much richer foundational structure.

This explains an important observation from the previous log: the absence of a notion from the surface of `Finlay` does not imply its absence from the complete dependency tree.

---

## Proof Compression

Reusable theorems compress mathematical reasoning.

At the level of the final theorem, a call to

```lean
MidsegmentTheorem
```

replaces an entire synthetic argument.

The same is true of a theorem expressing diagonal bisection in a parallelogram.

This compression is desirable: the final proof should expose its principal mathematical ideas rather than reproduce every foundational derivation.

But it creates a second task for library analysis.

To understand the logical strength of the final theorem, one must be able to expand these compressed nodes and trace them back to their assumptions.

---

## Dependency Graphs and Library Architecture

Dependency graphs therefore serve two different purposes.

First, they explain a proof mathematically: they show how one geometric structure is transformed into another.

Second, they expose the architecture of the library: they show where reusable results form boundaries between levels of abstraction.

A useful distinction is:

```text
proof graph
    =
high-level mathematical route

dependency graph
    =
full chain through interfaces, definitions, and axioms
```

The Finlay development initially gives us the first graph.

Reverse engineering aims to reconstruct the second.

---

## Consequences for Further Work

This distinction changes how the library should be investigated.

Instead of concluding immediately that midpoint theory or parallelogram theory must have a particular module structure, we can ask a more precise question:

> Which declarations form stable dependency boundaries that recur across independent proofs?

Only repeated evidence can justify turning such boundaries into permanent architectural modules.

The Finlay proof provides the first data point.

Further proofs will show which parts of its dependency structure are genuinely reusable and which are specific to this argument.

---

## Outlook

The next stage is to descend further through the dependency graph.

High-level synthetic theorems must be traced through their interfaces toward definitions and axioms.

The objective is to connect two views of the same development:

```text
mathematical proof structure
            |
            v
formal dependency structure
```

Understanding the relation between these two structures is becoming one of the central problems of the project.
