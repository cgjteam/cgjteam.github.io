# Log 010 - From Formal Proof to Geometry Atlas

## From Dependency Analysis to a Broader Program

### Introduction

The first phase of CGJteam Lab began with a deliberately limited objective: understand and verify one classical synthetic proof.

The chosen example was Ian Finlay's proof of the concurrency of the medians of a triangle.

Completing the formalization led to a sequence of further investigations. We began to inspect definitions, distinguish logical layers, and trace the dependencies hidden behind reusable theorems.

At this point a broader idea emerged.

Instead of treating each formal proof as an isolated result, geometric concepts could be studied systematically from several mathematical viewpoints.

This was the beginning of the Geometry Atlas idea.

---

## What the Finlay Experiment Contributed

The Finlay formalization provided more than the final theorem.

It exposed a collection of recurring concepts and mechanisms:

- midpoint relations,
- collinearity,
- parallelism,
- the Midsegment Theorem,
- parallelogram recognition,
- diagonal bisection,
- median construction.

The previous dependency analysis also showed that these notions cannot simply be placed on one logical level.

Some are definitions, some are derived results, and some are high-level interfaces that hide substantial lower-level reasoning.

This distinction is important for the Atlas.

The Atlas should describe mathematical concepts without confusing their geometric meaning with their status in one particular formalization.

---

## From Proof Dependencies to Mathematical Concepts

A dependency graph answers a local question:

> What does this proof use?

An atlas asks a different question:

> How can this geometric concept be understood across different theories and methods?

For example, midpoint can be approached through several perspectives:

- synthetic geometry,
- metric characterization,
- affine geometry,
- vector methods,
- transformation geometry,
- barycentric methods.

These descriptions need not have the same logical status in every axiomatic system.

The purpose of the Atlas is therefore comparative rather than foundational: to record how the same geometric object appears under different mathematical viewpoints.

---

## The Geometry Atlas Idea

The proposed Geometry Atlas is a collection of geometric concepts, configurations, and theorems studied from multiple perspectives.

A typical entry may record:

- the geometric object or configuration,
- its standard definitions or characterizations,
- important theorems associated with it,
- alternative proof methods,
- invariance under relevant transformations,
- connections with other parts of geometry,
- where appropriate, its formal representation in Lean.

The Atlas is therefore not intended to be identical with the Lean library.

The two projects interact, but they answer different questions.

The Lean development studies exact formal dependencies.

The Atlas studies the mathematical landscape surrounding the same objects.

---

## Several Viewpoints on Geometry

The Finlay experiment suggested that the same configuration can often be understood in substantially different ways.

For triangle geometry, useful viewpoints include:

### Synthetic

Reasoning directly with incidence, congruence, parallelism, circles, and classical constructions.

### Metric

Using lengths, angles, orthogonality, and metric identities.

### Affine and Vector

Studying properties preserved by affine transformations and expressing points through vector relations.

### Transformation-Theoretic

Understanding configurations through symmetries and transformation groups, in the spirit of Klein's Erlangen viewpoint.

### Barycentric

Representing points relative to a triangle and describing classical centers and configurations through barycentric coordinates.

These viewpoints overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

One purpose of the Atlas is to make their relationships visible.

---

## Finlay as the First Atlas Case

The concurrency of the medians provides a natural first example.

The synthetic Finlay proof proceeds through midpoint information, the Midsegment Theorem, parallelism, and an auxiliary parallelogram.

The same centroid configuration can also be studied by affine, vector, transformation-theoretic, or barycentric methods.

Thus the theorem that initiated the Lean project becomes a useful test case for a broader comparative study.

The point is no longer merely to accumulate alternative proofs.

The interesting question is what each proof reveals about the geometric structure of the configuration.

---

## Relation to Formalization

Lean remains important, but its role should be stated precisely.

Formalization can determine exact dependencies inside a chosen formal theory.

The Atlas can then place those dependencies beside other mathematical descriptions of the same object.

This creates two complementary directions:

```text
formalization
    |
    v
exact logical dependencies
```

and

```text
geometry atlas
    |
    v
comparison of mathematical viewpoints
```

Neither replaces the other.

Formalization provides logical precision; the Atlas provides mathematical comparison and context.

---

## Outlook

The Geometry Atlas idea emerged from the first formalization experiment, but it is broader than the Finlay proof and broader than the current Lean architecture.

Future entries can examine classical configurations from several viewpoints and compare the structures revealed by different proofs.

The immediate value of the Atlas is therefore exploratory.

It provides a place to ask not only whether a theorem can be proved, but also:

> What kind of geometry does this proof reveal?
