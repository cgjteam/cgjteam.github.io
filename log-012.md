# Log-012: Eliminating the Midsegment Axiom

**Date:** 2026-07-18  
**Project:** CGJteam Lab  
**Status:** Library Refactoring

---

## 1. Objective

The Midsegment Theorem had already been proved formally, but the existing geometry interface still exposed

```lean
axiom MidsegmentTheorem
```

to the rest of the development.

Thus there was a mismatch between the mathematical state of the project and its logical architecture: a result that was now available by proof was still being consumed as an axiom.

The objective was to remove this unnecessary assumption without breaking the existing Finlay development.

---

## 2. The Proved Theorem

The formal proof produced the theorem

```lean
MidsegmentParallel
```

in a form adapted to the geometry of the construction.

Its interface was not identical to the older `MidsegmentTheorem` declaration. In particular, the midpoint hypotheses occurred with different endpoint orientations.

Mathematically this difference is harmless. Formally it has to be accounted for explicitly.

---

## 3. Midpoint Symmetry

The required bridge was the theorem

```lean
MidpointSymmetry
```

expressing invariance of the midpoint relation under exchange of the segment endpoints.

Schematically:

```text
IsMidpoint M A B
        |
        v
IsMidpoint M B A
```

Once midpoint symmetry was available, hypotheses expected by `MidsegmentParallel` could be obtained from the form used by the existing interface.

This was a small technical step, but an important example of the distinction between mathematical equivalence and exact formal interfaces.

---

## 4. Preserving the Existing Interface

Rather than modifying every dependent proof, the existing theorem name and statement were retained:

```lean
theorem MidsegmentTheorem
```

Its implementation was changed.

Instead of being assumed axiomatically, it was derived from the proved `MidsegmentParallel`, together with the required midpoint symmetry.

The dependency therefore changed from

```text
axiom MidsegmentTheorem
        |
        v
Finlay
```

to

```text
proved MidsegmentParallel
        |
        v
compatibility theorem MidsegmentTheorem
        |
        v
Finlay
```

The high-level Finlay proof did not need to change.

---

## 5. Removing the Axiom

After the compatibility theorem had been introduced, the former

```lean
axiom MidsegmentTheorem
```

declaration could be removed.

The project was then recompiled.

The complete Finlay formalization continued to compile, confirming that the old axiom was no longer part of its dependency path.

This was the first concrete case in which a substantial geometric assumption used by the existing proof was replaced by a theorem proved inside the development.

---

## 6. What the Experiment Established

The main result was not merely that one declaration changed from `axiom` to `theorem`.

The experiment established a useful refactoring pattern:

1. prove the mathematical result independently,
2. compare its interface with the declaration already used by the library,
3. prove the minimal conversion lemmas required between the two forms,
4. reconstruct the old interface as a theorem,
5. remove the old axiom,
6. recompile dependent proofs.

This procedure separates two concerns:

- improving the logical foundations of the library,
- preserving a stable high-level API.

The distinction became important because foundational refactoring need not force simultaneous rewriting of every synthetic proof built above it.

---

## 7. A Necessary Qualification

Eliminating `MidsegmentTheorem` as an axiom does not mean that the Midsegment Theorem has become assumption-free.

Its proof still depends on the geometric axioms and lower-level results used by `MidsegmentParallel`.

What has been achieved is more precise:

> `MidsegmentTheorem` is no longer an independent primitive assumption.

Its logical content is now traced through a formal proof to lower-level assumptions.

This is exactly the kind of reduction sought in the reverse-engineering process.

---

## 8. Outlook

The next question is whether the same method can be applied to other declarations still treated axiomatically.

Each case must be analyzed separately.

Some axioms may turn out to be derivable from the existing foundations; others may represent genuine assumptions of the chosen geometry.

The objective is therefore not to eliminate axioms indiscriminately, but to distinguish derived geometric results from assumptions that genuinely belong to the foundational theory.
