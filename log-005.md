# Log 005 - Beyond the Proof

## Reflections after the First Complete Formalization

### Introduction

The original objective of the CGJteam Lab project was intentionally modest.

Rather than attempting to formalize a complete theory of synthetic geometry, we selected a single classical theorem: Ian Finlay's proof of the concurrency of the three medians of a triangle.

The goal was to understand the existing Lean geometry code, complete one substantial formal proof, and document the process.

This first objective has now been achieved.

The completed proof, however, revealed a second problem. Once the formalization worked, it became necessary to understand what had actually been built around it.

This log records that transition: from constructing a verified proof to examining the mathematical and logical architecture that emerged during its construction.

---

## From Proof to Questions

At the beginning of the project our attention was focused almost entirely on one question:

> Can this classical proof be formalized in Lean 4?

After several days of work the answer became positive.

The Lean code compiled and the proof was formally verified. But the resulting development contained auxiliary definitions, technical lemmas, intermediate results, and dependencies accumulated during the construction process.

Every element had been introduced for a reason. After the proof was completed, however, it became increasingly difficult to distinguish between several different roles:

- assumptions belonging to the underlying geometry,
- reusable mathematical theorems,
- interface lemmas introduced to make synthetic reasoning convenient,
- technical constructions required only by the formalization,
- artifacts of the particular route by which the proof had been developed.

This changed the character of the project.

Writing the verified proof had been the first problem.

Understanding the architecture of the resulting formal development became the second.

---

## Looking Beyond the Project

The questions raised by the Finlay formalization led naturally to a comparison with other approaches to formal geometry.

Hilbert's axiomatic organization, Tarski's deliberately small primitive language, and modern formalization projects illustrate substantially different ways of structuring geometric reasoning. Contemporary systems such as LeanGeo introduce still another emphasis: practical formal reasoning supported by higher-level interfaces and automation.

The comparison made one point especially clear: there is no single objective shared by all formal geometry projects.

A development may emphasize foundations, minimal axiomatization, reusable theorem libraries, automation, collaboration, or applications to theorem proving. These goals lead naturally to different architectures.

For our project, the immediate consequence was methodological. Rather than adopting one existing architecture wholesale, we needed to determine which structures had actually emerged from the proof we had formalized and which of them deserved to become stable parts of the library.

---

## The Architectural Question

After completing Finlay's proof, the central questions became:

- Which definitions are fundamental?
- Which lemmas express genuine reusable mathematics?
- Which declarations merely support the proof assistant?
- Which dependencies are logically essential?
- Which dependencies are historical artifacts of the construction process?
- Where should the boundary between axioms, interface results, and proof-specific arguments be placed?

These questions cannot be answered simply by observing that the final theorem compiles.

A successful proof establishes correctness of the argument under the stated assumptions. It does not by itself explain whether the surrounding formal architecture is mathematically natural or reusable.

The next stage therefore requires reading the formal development in the opposite direction: not constructing a proof from available components, but reconstructing the role and logical origin of the components from the completed proof.

---

## Understanding an Existing Formal Library

A formal library is not only a collection of proved theorems.

Its internal organization reflects choices about primitive notions, axioms, definitions, interfaces, theorem dependencies, and the level at which mathematical arguments are expressed.

Before extending such a library, these choices have to be understood.

In this sense, formalization creates a particular form of mathematical reading. Studying the code means reconstructing the mathematical architecture hidden behind definitions and theorem dependencies.

The Finlay experiment provided a small but concrete setting in which this problem could be studied.

The library was still at an early stage, but enough structure had already accumulated to make reverse analysis necessary.

---

## A Change of Method

The project therefore entered a new phase.

The immediate objective was no longer simply to add further theorems. The existing development first had to be examined structurally.

The intended procedure became:

1. inspect the definitions and assumptions used by the completed proof,
2. trace the logical dependencies of the important lemmas,
3. distinguish foundational assumptions from derived results,
4. identify reusable mathematical interfaces,
5. separate proof-specific constructions from general geometry,
6. reorganize the library only after these dependencies are understood.

This reverses the direction of the original work.

The first phase proceeded from geometric ideas toward a compiling Lean proof.

The new phase proceeds from the existing Lean proof back toward its mathematical and logical structure.

---

## Outlook

The next stage of CGJteam Lab will combine two activities:

1. formalization of classical synthetic arguments,
2. analysis and refinement of the reusable structures revealed by those formalizations.

The immediate task is to understand the geometry library already produced by the Finlay development before extending it substantially.

The longer-term objective remains the construction of a coherent environment in which synthetic geometry can be expressed, verified, and reorganized without losing sight of the logical origin of its results.
