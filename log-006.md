# Log 006 - Reverse Engineering the Geometry Core

## Understanding the Logical Structure Behind a Formal Geometry Library

### Introduction

The completion of Finlay's synthetic proof marked the first milestone of the CGJteam Lab project.

The formal proof was correct, the Lean code compiled, and the main mathematical argument had been transferred into a proof assistant.

Its completion, however, revealed a new problem.

A formal library is not understood simply by reading the final theorem. Its structure is distributed across definitions, primitive relations, auxiliary lemmas, interfaces, and dependencies between them.

The next stage of the project therefore changed direction.

Instead of immediately adding new geometric results, the focus moved toward reverse engineering the existing geometry code.

---

## From Building Proofs to Reading Libraries

A classical mathematical proof is usually presented as a sequence of ideas.

A formal proof contains additional information:

- exact logical dependencies,
- chosen definitions,
- required assumptions,
- interfaces between different parts of the theory.

This information is often invisible in ordinary mathematical writing.

The theorem

```lean
Finlay
```

states a familiar geometric result, but its formal realization depends on a chain of concepts involving midpoint relations, collinearity, parallelism, intersections, parallelograms, diagonal bisection, and medians.

The first task of reverse engineering was therefore not to add new results, but to determine where these notions came from and what logical role they played.

---

## What Belongs to the Core?

The initial inspection raised a basic architectural question:

> Which geometric notions are primitive, which are defined, and which belong only to a higher-level interface?

At the beginning of this investigation these levels were not yet clearly separated.

Relations such as collinearity, parallelism, betweenness, congruence, midpoint, median, and parallelogram all appeared in the formal development, but their logical status could not be inferred merely from their use in the Finlay proof.

This distinction became one of the main objects of the investigation.

The relevant questions were:

- Is a relation primitive or derived?
- Does a definition belong to the foundational geometry or to a convenient interface?
- Is a theorem a genuine consequence of the axioms, or has it been introduced only to support a particular proof?
- Which parts of the current organization reflect mathematics, and which reflect the history of implementation?

At this stage these were questions rather than settled architectural conclusions.

---

## The Midpoint Investigation

One of the first notions examined was the midpoint.

In elementary geometry, saying that a point is the midpoint of a segment combines several ideas: incidence with the segment, betweenness, and equality of the two resulting parts.

The formal development showed that a proof may use only selected consequences of this concept.

This made midpoint a useful test case for the reverse-engineering process.

Instead of assuming that its familiar mathematical meaning determined its formal status, we needed to inspect how midpoint was defined, which properties were assumed, and which were derived.

The same question would later recur for other geometric notions.

---

## Collinearity and the Form of Geometric Information

The role of collinearity was particularly visible during the Finlay formalization.

Informal geometry routinely changes the order in which collinear points are named. In Lean, the exact orientation of a relation matters because a later theorem may expect a different arrangement of arguments.

For example, a statement of the form

```lean
Geo.Collinear C G F
```

may have to be transformed into

```lean
Geo.Collinear C F G
```

before it can be used by another result.

This is mathematically elementary, but formally significant.

The example revealed an important distinction between a geometric fact and the particular logical form in which that fact is available to the proof assistant.

It also raised a further question: should such transformations be primitive assumptions, derived theorems, or part of a reusable interface?

---

## Parallelism and Orientation

Parallelism provided a similar example.

The development represents a parallel relation through ordered point arguments, so changes of orientation have to be handled explicitly.

Operations expressing symmetry or reversal of a parallel relation are therefore important for practical synthetic reasoning.

But reverse engineering changes the question we ask about them.

The issue is no longer merely whether such operations are convenient. We must determine their logical origin:

- are they axiomatic,
- are they consequences of more primitive assumptions,
- or are they interface theorems derived once and then reused?

This distinction is essential if the library is to expose the actual dependency structure of the geometry rather than only a convenient proof language.

---

## Discovering Layers

The first inspection suggested that the development should not be regarded as one undifferentiated collection of declarations.

A provisional picture began to emerge:

```text
foundational geometric notions
        |
        v
derived structural properties
        |
        v
reusable geometric interfaces
        |
        v
complete synthetic arguments
```

At this stage this was not yet a final architecture.

It was a working hypothesis produced by reading the completed proof backwards.

The important observation was that declarations used by a proof can occupy very different logical levels even when they appear side by side in Lean code.

Recognizing these levels became a prerequisite for any later reorganization of the library.

---

## From Construction to Reverse Engineering

The Finlay proof had originally been developed in the forward direction:

```text
geometric construction
        |
        v
auxiliary lemmas
        |
        v
formal proof
```

The new investigation proceeds in the opposite direction:

```text
formal proof
        |
        v
dependency analysis
        |
        v
logical classification
        |
        v
library architecture
```

This reversal is the main methodological result recorded in this log.

The completed proof becomes evidence from which the structure of the underlying formal theory can be reconstructed.

---

## Outlook

The next stage of the project is therefore to inspect the existing declarations more systematically.

The immediate goals are:

1. identify the genuinely primitive geometric notions,
2. trace important results back to their assumptions,
3. distinguish axioms from derived theorems,
4. separate foundational structures from higher-level interfaces,
5. postpone architectural reorganization until these dependencies are understood.

The investigation is still at an early stage.

The purpose of this log is not to present a finished geometry core, but to record the point at which understanding the logical structure of the existing formalization became a research problem in its own right.
