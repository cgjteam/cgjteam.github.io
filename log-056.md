# Log 056 --- Euclid Book XI: Synthetic Spatial Geometry in Progress

## 1. Status of this log

This is a living project log.

The current production checkpoint is Proposition XI.17.  Euclid's Book XI
contains 39 propositions, so the formal reconstruction has passed the
one-third point and is approaching the halfway mark, but the book is not yet
complete.

This log is therefore different from Logs 054 and 055.

Those logs summarize completed Books I and II.

Log 056 records the architecture that has emerged so far in Book XI and is
intended to grow as XI.18 and the later propositions are formalized.

The current scope is

```text
Euclid Book XI
XI.1 -- XI.17
```

with the expectation that later revisions of this same log will extend the
mathematical picture rather than replace it.

## 2. Why Book XI changes the project

Book I tested the planar synthetic geometry library.

Book II tested a synthetic calculus of geometric content.

Book XI tests something qualitatively different:

```text
genuine three-dimensional incidence
carrier planes
intersections of planes
spatial perpendicularity
spatial parallelism
plane sections
transport between ambient space and plane slices
```

The main difficulty is not that individual classical arguments are always
long.

Many are very short.

The difficulty is that a two-dimensional diagram can silently identify its
ambient plane, while a solid-geometry diagram cannot.

In three dimensions one must continually know:

```text
which points lie in which plane,
which line is contained in which plane,
which two planes determine a line,
whether two disjoint lines are coplanar or skew,
and in which plane a planar theorem is being applied.
```

Lean forces these questions into the proof state.

That is one of the principal mathematical lessons of the reconstruction.

## 3. The foundation after the Hilbert refactor

The Hilbert architecture has been reorganized while Book XI was already in
development.

The source-faithful foundation is now

```text
HilbertCore
    |
HilbertAxioms
```

where `HilbertAxioms.lean` records Hilbert's Groups I--V in source order.

In particular, the spatial incidence clauses I.4--I.8 are part of the same
source-level Group I as the planar incidence clauses.

There is no separate source-faithful 3D axiom foundation.

The planar route continues through

```text
HilbertAxioms
    |
HilbertPlaneAPI
    |
HilbertPlaneTheory
    |
HilbertGrundlagen
    |
HilbertInterface
    |
HilbertBookZero
    |
Book I
```

while the spatial route passes through

```text
HilbertAxioms
    |
HilbertSpaceIncidence
    |
Hilbert3DInterface
    |
Book XI
```

These two routes interact constantly because a Book XI proof often enters an
explicit plane slice and then reuses the established planar theory.

## 4. The spatial compatibility boundary

`HilbertSpaceIncidence.lean` currently has two roles.

First, it contains genuine derived spatial incidence results proved from the
source-faithful Hilbert Group I.

Second, it preserves the existing downstream interface

```text
HilbertSpacePrimitive
HilbertSpaceIncidence
HilbertSpaceOrder
HilbertSpaceCongruence
HilbertSpaceEuclidean
```

so that the already developed Book XI proofs did not have to be rewritten
simultaneously with the foundational refactor.

This distinction matters.

The surviving `HilbertSpace*` names in theorem signatures are current
compatibility interfaces.

They are not evidence for a second independent Hilbert axiom system.

That separation is now recorded explicitly in the Book XI documentation.

## 5. PlaneGeo is the basic proof mechanism

A recurring Book XI pattern is

```text
ambient three-space
        |
        v
construct or identify a carrier plane
        |
        v
PlaneGeo on that plane
        |
        v
apply planar Hilbert / Book I geometry
        |
        v
transport the conclusion back to ambient space
```

This is not merely an implementation device.

It expresses a mathematical distinction that is easy to blur on paper.

Order, angle, Pasch, same-side, alternate-angle, and many perpendicularity
arguments are intrinsically planar.

The ambient space supplies the incidence structure needed to locate the
correct plane.

Once that plane has been identified, the existing planar library should be
reused rather than duplicated in a new 3D form.

## 6. XI.1--XI.3: the incidence layer

The first three propositions establish the basic language in which solid
geometry can be discussed.

XI.1 says that if two distinct points of a line lie in a plane, then the whole
line lies in that plane.

XI.2 establishes the carrier plane of two intersecting lines and therefore of
a nondegenerate triangle.

XI.3 identifies the intersection of two distinct intersecting planes as a
line.

These propositions are mathematically elementary, but they expose the
dimension-sensitive incidence structure required by everything that follows.

They also show an important difference between Euclid's organization and the
formal library.

Some statements that Euclid proves as early propositions of Book XI are
already natural consequences of the Hilbert incidence layer.

The formal proposition can therefore become a thin wrapper around a general
spatial theorem.

## 7. XI.4: the first major spatial theorem

XI.4 is the first large test of the spatial architecture.

Its hypothesis is local:

```text
a line through O is perpendicular
to two intersecting lines of a plane through O
```

but its conclusion is universal:

```text
the line is perpendicular to the plane,
hence to every line of that plane through O.
```

The classical diagram makes this transition look almost immediate.

The formal proof does not.

It must reconstruct the arbitrary-transversal argument, keep the required
Pasch reasoning inside explicit plane slices, and compare triangles that may
live in different carrier planes.

The result is still completely synthetic.

No coordinates, vectors, scalar product, or angle measure are introduced.

XI.4 becomes the first major reusable theorem of the Book XI development.

## 8. XI.5--XI.7: coplanarity before parallelism

XI.5 turns perpendicularity information into coplanarity.

This is a genuinely spatial move.

XI.6 then proves that two lines perpendicular to the same plane are parallel.

In three-dimensional geometry this statement has two logically separate
parts:

```text
first prove coplanarity,
then prove planar nonintersection.
```

This distinction is essential.

Two ambient lines can be disjoint and still be skew.

The formal definition of spatial parallelism therefore records both
coplanarity and disjointness.

XI.7 then exploits this stronger notion: once two parallel lines come with a
common carrier plane, the joining line of selected points can be placed in the
same plane by the spatial line-in-plane principle.

The sequence

```text
XI.4
  |
  v
XI.5
  |
  v
XI.6
  |
  v
XI.7
```

is one of the first clear structural chains of the book.

## 9. XI.8: the Group IV boundary

XI.8 is the converse companion to XI.6.

It starts with two parallel spatial lines and assumes that one is
perpendicular to a plane.

The conclusion is that the second parallel is perpendicular to the same
plane.

This is the first point in the current Book XI development where Hilbert
Group IV is mathematically required.

The distinction is useful:

```text
XI.4 -- XI.7
neutral spatial geometry

XI.8
Euclidean parallel uniqueness enters
```

The formal proof separates the neutral part from the Euclidean uniqueness
step rather than hiding both under one undifferentiated notion of parallel
lines.

## 10. XI.9 and XI.10: spatial parallelism acquires direction

XI.9 is the spatial counterpart of the transitivity phenomenon familiar from
Euclid I.30.

The new issue is that ambient lines need not all lie in one plane.

The proof must therefore control the carrier planes that connect the three
parallel lines.

XI.10 goes one step further.

Ordinary unoriented spatial parallelism is no longer enough.

The proposition compares angles formed by corresponding parallel lines in
different planes, so the formalization must remember which rays correspond.

This led to an explicitly directed spatial parallel relation.

The progression is conceptually important:

```text
disjointness
    is not enough

coplanar disjointness
    gives spatial parallelism

spatial parallelism
    is not enough for XI.10

directed spatial parallelism
    controls the angle correspondence
```

This is a good example of formalization exposing information that a classical
diagram carries implicitly.

## 11. XI.11--XI.13: existence and uniqueness of normals

XI.11 constructs a perpendicular to a plane from a point outside the plane.

XI.12 constructs a perpendicular through a point lying in the plane.

XI.13 supplies the corresponding uniqueness phenomenon.

This block heavily exercises the plane-space interface.

The construction repeatedly moves between ambient geometry and explicitly
chosen plane slices.

Planar perpendicular constructions are performed where they belong: inside a
plane.

Spatial theorems such as XI.4 and XI.8 then promote the local result back to a
line perpendicular to the ambient plane.

The architecture is therefore not

```text
invent a separate three-dimensional version
of every planar construction
```

but rather

```text
use spatial incidence to choose the right plane,
reuse planar geometry there,
then transport the result.
```

This principle is becoming one of the most stable design rules of the project.

## 12. XI.14 and XI.15: parallel planes emerge

XI.14 moves from perpendicular lines to parallel planes.

If the same line is perpendicular to two planes, those planes are parallel.

The classical contradiction uses a hypothetical intersection point and a
triangle with two right angles.

The formal proof preserves that idea but must explicitly separate degenerate
cases, construct the auxiliary carrier plane, and track the relevant
perpendicularity witnesses.

XI.15 then gives a more structural criterion.

Two planes containing corresponding intersecting pairs of parallel lines are
parallel.

At this point the architecture developed since XI.4 begins to behave as a
coherent theory rather than a collection of proposition-local devices.

The proof can combine:

```text
XI.11  existence of a normal
XI.4   criterion for line-plane perpendicularity
XI.14  parallelism of planes perpendicular to one line
Book I parallel/perpendicular transport
```

into a recognizably Euclidean argument.

## 13. XI.16: the section theorem

XI.16 is mathematically simple but structurally central.

If two parallel planes are cut by a third plane, their section lines in the
third plane are parallel.

The proof is almost pure incidence.

If the two section lines met, their intersection point would lie in both
parallel planes.

This theorem is important because it isolates a reusable operation:

```text
parallel ambient planes
        +
transversal plane
        |
        v
parallel section lines
```

That operation becomes the spatial engine for XI.17 and is also exactly the
kind of statement naturally expressed by a flat/carrier calculus.

## 14. XI.17: the first proportion theorem

XI.17 is the current endpoint of the canonical Book XI development.

Three parallel planes cut two transversals:

```text
A - E - B
C - F - D
```

and Euclid concludes that the two transversals are cut in the same ratio.

This is the first current proposition where genuinely spatial geometry and
classical proportion theory interact essentially.

The canonical route separates the problem into two layers.

First comes the three-dimensional geometry:

```text
parallel planes
        |
        v
XI.16 section parallelism
        |
        v
construction of the auxiliary point O
        |
        v
two explicit planar slices
```

Then comes the proportion calculation:

```text
VI.2 in the first plane
VI.2 in the second plane
V.11-style transitivity
```

The separation is useful because it shows exactly where the spatial problem
ends and the proportion problem begins.

## 15. The Wyler path changes the interpretation of XI.17

The independent Wyler reconstruction of XI.17 gives a stronger architectural
lesson.

The spatial configuration can be expressed using:

```text
generated planes
exact carrier meets
parallel section lines
Pasch
```

and the final invariant can be taken to be the affine relation

```text
HilbertWylerSameDivision
```

rather than a metric segment-ratio relation.

The production endpoint

```text
euclid_proposition_11_17_wyler_affine
```

does not assume the plane-local `HilbertVI2Raw` interface and does not pass
through the right-triangle/raw-ratio machinery.

This is important because it reveals that the geometric content of XI.17 is
more affine than its classical ratio notation initially suggests.

The three parallel planes determine the same division position on different
transversals.

The proportion language is one representation of that fact.

The Wyler relation is another, more structural one.

## 16. Book XI is becoming a laboratory for flat geometry

The work on XI.16 and XI.17 has begun to connect the Euclid development with
the separate Wyler and Smith investigations.

The relevant objects are increasingly:

```text
planes generated by data,
their exact intersections,
section lines,
carrier uniqueness,
and transport of incidence information between flats.
```

This is significant beyond Book XI.

The same language appears naturally in the synthetic analysis of reflection
groups and in higher-dimensional geometry.

Book XI is therefore doing more than extending the Euclid formalization into
space.

It is supplying concrete configurations in which a flat calculus can be
tested against classical geometry.

## 17. What Lean makes visible in solid geometry

Several recurring hidden obligations have now become clear.

### Carrier planes

A classical proof often says "in the plane containing..." and proceeds.

Lean requires the plane to be constructed or recovered from previous data.

### Skew lines

In a planar proof, disjoint lines are candidates for parallelism.

In space, disjointness is insufficient.

Coplanarity must be proved separately.

### Plane intersections

A line described as "the intersection of two planes" carries more data than a
bare line.

Later arguments need proofs that the line lies in each plane.

### Orientation

XI.10 shows that unoriented parallelism can be too weak.

Corresponding rays may have to be tracked explicitly.

### Hidden existence

Euclid may simply name an intersection point.

Formalization must prove that the relevant line and plane, or two lines inside
a plane, actually meet.

These are not bureaucratic annoyances.

They are parts of the logical content of three-dimensional synthetic geometry.

## 18. The role of Book I is changing

Book I remains indispensable, but its role is different from the role it had
inside Book II.

In Book XI a Book I theorem is often used inside an induced plane:

```text
ambient 3D configuration
        |
        v
choose PlaneGeo(sigma)
        |
        v
apply I.12, I.17, I.27, I.29, I.31, I.33, ...
        |
        v
transport back to space
```

This makes Book I function as a reusable planar engine inside a larger
spatial theory.

The completion of Book I was therefore not merely a prerequisite in the
chronological sense.

It created a library that can now be instantiated repeatedly on different
planes inside one three-dimensional proof.

## 19. A clearer distinction between mathematical and module dependencies

Book XI makes it especially dangerous to confuse three different graphs:

```text
Euclid's proposition dependency graph

the mathematical dependency graph
of the reconstructed proof

the Lean module import graph
```

They are related but not identical.

For example, a classical proof may cite an earlier Book XI proposition, while
the Lean proof may use a more general spatial theorem already extracted into
the interface.

Conversely, a Lean module may import a proposition because it provides useful
infrastructure even when the classical proof does not name that proposition.

The documentation therefore keeps local dependency diagrams mathematical.

The complete module architecture is recorded globally rather than expanded in
every proposition chapter.

## 20. What the Hilbert refactor has tested

Book XI was already substantial when the Hilbert source layer was reorganized.

That made the refactor itself a useful experiment.

The source-faithful Groups I--V could be separated from the older compatibility
interfaces without rewriting the Book XI theorem bodies.

This indicates that the project has begun to acquire real abstraction
boundaries.

The current picture is:

```text
source-faithful Hilbert axioms
        |
        v
compatibility / transport layers
        |
        v
derived planar and spatial interfaces
        |
        v
Euclid proposition proofs
```

The proposition proofs should depend on mathematical interfaces, not on the
accidental historical location of the axioms in source files.

## 21. What has not been claimed

The present checkpoint should not be mistaken for a completion of Book XI.

Several boundaries remain open.

The canonical XI.17 route still uses a raw plane-local VI.2 interface for its
proportion calculation.

The Wyler affine route removes that dependency for XI.17 itself, but this does
not by itself reconstruct the whole of Euclidean proportion theory.

Nor has the remaining sequence XI.18--XI.39 yet been tested against the
current spatial architecture.

Later propositions may require additional reusable ideas, especially as the
book moves further into:

```text
solid angles,
parallelopipeds,
equal solids,
and the later volume theory of Book XI.
```

This log must therefore remain provisional.

## 24. Provisional conclusion

Through XI.17, Book XI has already changed the geometry library.

The central achievement so far is not a collection of seventeen isolated
formal theorems.

It is the emergence of a reusable synthetic language for three-dimensional
reasoning:

```text
ambient incidence
carrier planes
PlaneGeo slices
plane intersections
spatial perpendicularity
coplanar-disjoint parallelism
directed spatial parallels
parallel planes
section lines
affine division across parallel sections
```

The work is incomplete, but the architecture is no longer experimental in the
same sense as at XI.1.

The first part of Book XI has begun to show how classical solid geometry can
be reconstructed without coordinates while still exposing the information
that Euclid's diagrams leave implicit.

The remaining propositions will test how far this architecture scales.
