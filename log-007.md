# Log 007 - Definitions Before Theorems

## Introduction

The previous stage began the reverse engineering of the geometry code used by the Finlay formalization.

A more precise question now appeared:

> Which notions occurring in the proof are primitive, which are defined, and which belong to a higher-level interface?

This question cannot be answered simply by listing the geometric concepts used in the final theorem.

The purpose of this log is therefore not to present a finished geometry core, but to record the investigation of definitions that preceded the analysis of theorems and their dependencies.

---

## Reading Definitions First

When a formal proof is read from the final theorem backwards, many notions initially appear on the same level.

The Finlay development uses concepts such as:

- collinearity,
- parallelism,
- betweenness,
- congruence,
- midpoint,
- median,
- parallelogram.

Their simultaneous appearance in a proof does not imply that they have the same logical status.

Some may belong to the foundational language. Others may be definitions constructed from more primitive relations. Still others may be convenient interfaces introduced for synthetic reasoning.

The first task is therefore classification rather than extension.

Before asking what can be proved from the library, we must determine what the library is actually assuming and defining.

---

## Relations Instead of Coordinates

The development is synthetic in the sense that its proofs are expressed through geometric relations rather than coordinate calculations.

For the Finlay argument, the relevant statements concern facts such as:

- points being collinear,
- segments determining parallel directions,
- points satisfying midpoint conditions,
- a quadrilateral satisfying parallelogram conditions,
- diagonals producing midpoint information.

No coordinate computation is needed in the proof itself.

This observation is useful, but it does not yet determine which of these notions should be primitive.

That is a separate logical question, and one of the purposes of the reverse-engineering process is precisely to answer it.

---

## Midpoint as a Test Case

Midpoint provides a useful example of the distinction.

Informally, saying that \(M\) is the midpoint of \(AB\) combines several geometric ideas: incidence with the line or segment, betweenness, and equality of the two parts.

In Lean, however, the relevant question is more precise:

> What is the formal definition of midpoint, and which properties of that definition are actually used later?

The Finlay proof uses midpoint information in connection with collinearity, the Midsegment Theorem, and the identification of a median.

This makes midpoint an appropriate test case for distinguishing:

```text
primitive relation
        |
        v
definition
        |
        v
derived property
        |
        v
reusable theorem
```

The important point is not that midpoint is minimal or primitive, but that its logical status has to be reconstructed from the code.

---

## Absence from a Proof Is Not Absence from the Theory

The Finlay proof does not visibly use many familiar tools of Euclidean geometry, such as coordinates, explicit distance calculations, circle constructions, or triangle similarity.

This is useful information about the proof.

It tells us that these notions are not required in the particular dependency path currently being studied.

It does **not** by itself show that they are absent from the underlying geometry, nor that the complete theory can be developed without them.

This distinction is important.

Reverse engineering must separate:

- what a particular theorem uses,
- what the library provides,
- what the axiomatic theory assumes,
- what may be required elsewhere.

A dependency path through one theorem is evidence about that theorem, not automatically a characterization of the entire geometry.

---

## From a Flat List to Logical Layers

The initial inspection suggests that the declarations occurring in the formalization belong to different logical layers.

A provisional picture is:

```text
primitive notions and relations
        |
        v
defined geometric concepts
        |
        v
derived structural results
        |
        v
reusable synthetic interfaces
        |
        v
complete geometric proofs
```

This is deliberately more cautious than treating midpoint, parallelism, median, and parallelogram as equally primitive components of one `Geo` structure.

At this stage the purpose is to discover the actual boundaries between these layers.

Those boundaries must be established by inspecting definitions and dependencies, not inferred from mathematical familiarity or from the order in which declarations happen to appear in a file.

---

## Consequences for the Investigation

The reverse-engineering procedure therefore begins with definitions.

For each important notion we need to ask:

1. Where is it introduced?
2. Is it primitive or defined?
3. If defined, from which earlier notions?
4. Which elementary properties are proved from the definition?
5. Which later theorems depend on it?

Only after this classification does it become meaningful to discuss a stable geometry API or a final core architecture.

This changes the immediate goal of the project.

The task is not yet to design the smallest possible foundation. It is to reconstruct the foundation already implicit in the formal development.

---

## Outlook

The next step is to continue this analysis from definitions to theorems and axioms.

The expected result is not merely a shorter list of primitives, but a dependency structure showing how higher-level synthetic concepts arise from lower-level assumptions.

The architecture of the geometry library should be a consequence of that analysis rather than an assumption made in advance.
