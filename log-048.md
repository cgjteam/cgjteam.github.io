# Log 048 --- Diagrams as Proof Analysis

## From illustration to a diagnostic tool

The documentation audit of the first part of Euclid's Book I began as a
practical editorial task.

Some proposition documents contained too many figures. Several figures
were successive snapshots of the same construction. Others were crowded,
poorly spaced, or did not clearly distinguish the geometric data that
mattered.

The initial objective was therefore modest:

> make the diagrams cleaner and more useful.

The result was more interesting.

Once the diagrams were compared systematically with Euclid's proof, the
Lean source, and modern commentary, they began to expose mathematical
information that was easy to miss in prose alone.

The graphical audit became a proof audit.

## 1. A diagram should have a mathematical job

The first change was to stop treating every construction instruction as
a reason for another picture.

A sequence such as

``` text
draw segment
draw first circle
draw second circle
mark intersection
join two points
```

does not necessarily require five diagrams.

If every picture differs from the previous one only by one added object,
the reader receives an animation of the construction rather than an
analysis of the proof.

The working rule that emerged is:

> A figure should represent a geometrically distinct configuration or
> clarify a logically significant step.

This immediately reduced the number of diagrams in several proposition
documents.

For example, Proposition I.2 originally used a long sequence of
construction snapshots. The revised presentation needs only:

``` text
Figure 1: the input data
Figure 2: the complete classical construction
```

The proof text explains the intermediate operations.

The same principle applies to I.5, where several successive pictures of
the same extended isosceles triangle were replaced by one complete
classical configuration.

## 2. Metric markings should encode information, not decoration

The audit also changed the use of side marks and angle marks.

A diagram becomes difficult to read when every equality mentioned in the
proof is encoded graphically at once.

The useful distinction is between:

``` text
data that define the configuration
```

and

``` text
facts that the proof subsequently derives
```

The first category is often worth marking directly.

For example, in a triangle-congruence proposition the corresponding
input sides can be marked with one, two, or three ticks.

Derived equalities need not all be added to the same figure.

This keeps the diagram from becoming a second proof written in symbols
on top of the geometry.

The audit also exposed a purely typographical rule: congruence marks
must visibly belong to the segments they describe. Marks floating beside
an edge, or packed into a cluster near a vertex, are worse than no marks
at all.

## 3. Proposition I.1: the familiar picture hides an existence theorem

The two-circle diagram for Proposition I.1 is perhaps the clearest
example of a picture revealing a foundational issue.

The classical construction is familiar:

``` text
        C
       / \
      /   \
     A-----B
```

with one circle centered at (A) through (B) and another centered at (B)
through (A).

Visually, the point (C) appears unavoidable.

Logically, it is not.

Drawing the two circles does not by itself prove that they have a common
point. The classical picture silently presents the required intersection
as already available.

Modern commentary on Euclid makes this gap explicit: some
circle-intersection or continuity principle is needed.

The diagram therefore performs two different roles.

For Euclid it is the construction picture.

For foundational analysis it is evidence of an unstated existence step.

The Lean reconstruction makes the difference especially visible because
it does not use circle intersection at all:

``` text
hilbert_equidistant_point_exists
        |
equidistant witness C
        |
prove C is not collinear with A,B
```

The diagram and the formal proof expose different foundational burdens.

## 4. Proposition I.2: extension and intersection are not automatic

Proposition I.2 gives a second example.

Euclid produces sides of an equilateral triangle until they meet circles
introduced later in the construction.

A finished diagram makes those intersections look harmless because all
the required objects are already present on the page.

But the construction requires more than the permission to draw a line
and a circle.

One must know that the produced line reaches the circle and that the
relevant intersection point exists.

The completed diagram therefore hides two kinds of work:

``` text
extension far enough
        +
line-circle intersection
```

This is another reason not to interpret classical construction diagrams
as self-validating objects.

They record the intended configuration. They do not automatically
establish its existence from the stated axioms.

## 5. Proposition I.4: superposition has a side choice

The diagrammatic audit of Proposition I.4 exposed a different issue.

SAS is often represented simply by drawing two triangles with two pairs
of equal sides and an equal included angle.

Euclid's superposition proof requires more.

When one triangle is applied to the other, the transported third vertex
must be placed on the appropriate side of the target base ray.

Schematically:

``` text
place A on D
place ray AB on ray DE
        |
B coincides with E
        |
choose the correct side of DE
        |
ray AC coincides with ray DF
        |
C coincides with F
```

The same-side choice is easy to overlook when the reader sees only two
finished triangles.

Here the diagram audit did not discover a missing theorem. It exposed a
positional condition implicit in the operation of superposition.

## 6. Proposition I.7: one picture is not the theorem

Proposition I.7 produced the strongest warning against treating a
familiar diagram as if it represented all cases.

The proposition excludes two distinct points on the same side of a base
that have the same distances from the two endpoints of the base.

A standard Euclidean diagram shows one placement of the two candidate
vertices.

But the possible position of the second vertex is not exhausted by that
picture.

Modern analysis of the proposition separates several regions in the
relevant half-plane. Euclid's preserved argument explicitly treats only
one of the positional cases.

This changed the documentation policy for I.7.

The figure is retained, but its caption identifies it as a
representative Euclidean configuration rather than a universal picture
of the theorem.

That distinction matters:

``` text
diagram of one proof case
        !=
diagram of the full logical domain
```

The Hilbert proof reinforces the point because it avoids the region
analysis through angle and segment uniqueness.

The graphical limitation of the classical proof and the different
architecture of the formal proof become visible together.

## 7. Proposition I.8: diagrams can show what superposition needs

Proposition I.8 again uses superposition.

A useful diagram is not merely a pair of triangles with three side
congruences. The second figure should isolate the decisive configuration
after one triangle has been placed on the other.

At that stage the role of Proposition I.7 becomes visible: if the
transported third vertex differed from the target vertex while
satisfying the same two distance conditions on the same side of the
base, I.7 would be violated.

Thus two figures have genuinely different jobs:

``` text
Figure 1:
the SSS input data

Figure 2:
the superposition configuration in which I.7 acts
```

This is a good example of when multiple figures are justified.

They do not show successive drawing instructions. They show different
logical states of the argument.

## 8. A graphical audit can correct the prose

The process also revealed that figure review cannot be separated
completely from proof review.

When a figure is difficult to explain accurately, one of several things
may be wrong:

``` text
the figure is badly designed;
the caption does not state its role;
the prose attributes too much to the picture;
the documented proof does not match the source code;
a hidden case or hypothesis has been omitted.
```

This happened repeatedly during the audit.

The practical consequence is that figures should not be reviewed only
for appearance.

For each figure we now ask:

``` text
What proposition or proof step does this picture represent?

Which relations are hypotheses?

Which relations are conclusions?

Does the picture represent one case or all cases?

Does the Lean proof actually use this configuration?

Would removing the figure lose mathematical information?
```

These questions turn graphical editing into proof analysis.

## 9. Wilkins as a control source

The study notes of David Wilkins became particularly useful during this
stage.

Their value was not that the reconstruction should copy Wilkins's
diagrams.

That would simply replace one visual convention by another.

Instead, the notes provide a control source for questions such as:

``` text
What is the actual classical construction?

Which points and joins matter?

Is a positional case being suppressed?

Does Euclid rely on an implicit intersection?

Which previous proposition is doing the decisive work?
```

Sometimes the result is that a Wilkins-style feature should be added.

Sometimes the result is the opposite: several figures in the notes
correspond to material that is unnecessary for the present
reconstruction.

The principle is therefore:

> consult the classical source to understand the proof, then design the
> diagram required by this document.

## 10. The diagrams should not imitate the Lean proof either

There is an equal danger in the other direction.

Once the formal proof architecture is understood, it is tempting to draw
flowcharts for every theorem call and dependency.

That is not the job of the geometric figure.

The documentation now separates two visual languages.

### Geometric figures

TikZ is used for actual geometric configurations:

``` text
points
segments
rays
circles
angles
relative position
```

### Proof architecture

Simple ASCII diagrams are used for logical structure:

``` text
HilbertSSS
    |
angle congruence
    |
same-ray uniqueness
    |
segment uniqueness
```

This separation has improved both.

The geometry is no longer cluttered with dependency information, and the
proof architecture no longer requires elaborate TikZ flowcharts.

## 11. The same economy applies to code

The graphical audit led to a parallel editorial rule for Lean code.

A proposition document should not reproduce a large theorem body merely
because the code exists.

The repository already performs the archival function.

A code excerpt belongs in the document when it explains something:

``` text
a decisive interface theorem;
an unexpected change of proof architecture;
a definitional collapse;
a useful uniqueness principle;
a nontrivial formal reorientation.
```

The same standard now governs figures and code:

> include an object when it carries explanatory information.

This has made the proposition documents shorter while increasing the
amount of actual analysis.

## 12. A three-layer presentation

The emerging documentation style can be summarized as three
complementary representations.

### Geometry

``` text
TikZ figure
```

shows the mathematical configuration.

### Proof structure

``` text
ASCII dependency diagram
```

shows the architecture of the argument.

### Formal evidence

``` text
short Lean excerpt
```

shows the interface call or formal transition that matters.

None of the three should duplicate the others.

A good proposition document lets them answer different questions:

``` text
What is the configuration?
How does the proof move?
Where does Lean perform the decisive step?
```

## 13. Diagrams as tests of abstraction

There is a deeper connection with the Book Zero experiment.

A crowded diagram often indicates that too many low-level construction
steps are being represented simultaneously.

A clean formal proof often indicates that those steps have already been
packaged into a reusable operation.

For example:

``` text
Euclid I.2 diagram:
equilateral triangle
+ extensions
+ two circles
+ intersections

Book Zero:
layoff
```

The contrast is not an argument that the diagram is obsolete.

It shows what the abstraction has accomplished.

The classical diagram displays the mechanism.

The Book Zero theorem names the operation produced by that mechanism.

Both views are mathematically informative.

## 14. Current graphical standard

The audit of the first part of Book I has produced a practical standard
for the remaining proposition documents.

1.  Use a white, uncluttered background.
2.  Number figures and refer to them explicitly in the text.
3.  Do not create a new figure for every construction instruction.
4.  Use multiple figures only for genuinely distinct configurations or
    proof states.
5.  Put equality marks directly on the segments they describe.
6.  Do not overload a figure with every derived equality.
7.  Separate geometric TikZ figures from ASCII proof-architecture
    diagrams.
8.  State in the caption when a picture represents only one positional
    case.
9.  Check classical diagrams against the actual proof, not only against
    visual convention.
10. Check the documented formal architecture against the actual Lean
    source.

These rules are provisional, but they are now based on experience with
real Book I propositions rather than on a general preference for a
particular graphic style.

## Conclusion

The graphical revision of Book I began as document cleanup and became
part of the mathematical analysis.

The central lesson is:

> A diagram is not merely an illustration of a proof. It is a
> representation of selected geometric information, and the selection
> itself must be justified.

For the early propositions of Book I, diagram analysis exposed hidden
intersection assumptions, extension requirements, side choices in
superposition, and incomplete positional coverage.

At the same time, comparison with the Lean reconstruction showed which
of those classical mechanisms had been replaced by abstract existence,
congruence, order, or uniqueness theorems.

The result is a more economical graphical style, but also a more precise
account of what the reconstruction is actually doing.

The documentation has therefore become a third component of the project
alongside theorem proving and interface design: it is now one of the
tools by which the proof architecture itself is examined.
